When she buried her mother, the only thing left between her and her degree was Dimitriou: Topology II. It would be her third time sitting it, and her third time crying. She would save a few tears for her mom as she took the car and headed, generally, west. In the Prefecture of Imathia she would marvel at a few million mosquitoes swirling around an invisible cloud of humidity. In the Prefecture of Kozani she would see the bare mountains of Siatista, which the locals say are so clean because the women of Siatista have mopped them too. In Grevena she wouldn’t stop to eat, even though she’d be hungry, and on reaching Ioannina she would phone her brother to confirm that her mom had really died. She really had died. The other three times her mother had died, Chrysoula took her car and drove to her rugged village deep in Tzoumerka, and there her mom (oh, miracle!) was alive and simply missing her children. Chrysoula figured her mother would never really die, with so many supposed deaths behind her. Her mom’s fake deaths and Algebraic Topology were the only two constants in her life. All of that, of course, until her mom actually died, one constant went to hell, and Chrysoula headed for the utterly isolated and equally unfamiliar village in the most rugged part of Tzoumerka, on the border between the prefectures of Ioannina and Arta.

Her brother was waiting for her at the door of the house. Their father was inside, next to the ebony coffin, with his wife’s photograph propped against it. He wasn’t crying. He had cried enough in the previous hours and couldn’t manage more. There is always an upper limit to how much of something a person can do, and everything has a point where it turns toxic and dangerous. In Algebra, the upper bound is the largest value among all the values a function takes. Put simply, beyond that point the function is done for, it has no more values. Take the function Broccoli, for example. There is an upper value which, if you exceed it, can kill you. I dunno, how many kilos of broccoli does one have to eat to die, six, seven, eight? Because sorting foods mathematically into healthy and unhealthy doesn’t hold up, since over some range of values all foods are healthy and over another they’re lethal. With a suitable abstraction, broccoli is no different from pork cracklings. Thirty kilos of broccoli may be as deadly as one kilo of cracklings. I don’t know whether these values have ever been investigated, but Chrysoula had thought about it quite a few times. Because Algebra she knew. Algebraic Topology she didn’t. “One is one thing, the other is another,” she used to say.

Anyway, the bottom line is that her dad had cried enough, the upper bound had been reached, and it would be wise not to go beyond it. And he didn’t. He didn’t cry again that day, not when the coffin reached the church, and not when it was lowered on straps two meters into the earth of the village’s new cemetery, above the stream they had discovered was there when they tried to exhume the first residents of the cemetery a few years earlier and saw the dead staring them in the eyes, untouched by time and by whatever process should have taken place to make them one with the soil that raised them. Chrysoula’s mom was buried in that unwelcomingly protective earth, in the presence of the whole village, on a Wednesday afternoon. Chrysoula stayed in the village a few extra days with her dad and, once she was sure he could manage on his own, went back to Thessaloniki.

Chrysoula still hadn’t managed to finish the Mathematics degree, but along the way she had managed to finish the Geology Department, reasoning that teaching Geology at some school and earning a salary that let her live slightly above the poverty line was better than trying to finish Mathematics and living at the poverty line. She was thirty-four by now. Until a year earlier there had also been her father, who helped out with part of his pension. Her father no longer existed, he had been buried next to her mother. All the same, the debts he left behind were alive and kicking. The bank wouldn’t allow them to be buried, and they were automatically transferred to the children, so that the children could be buried too (up to the neck). The debts were split evenly. Eighty-six thousand four hundred and ninety euros in total for Takis and Chrysoula, “forty-three thousand two hundred and forty-five euros each,” the bank manager told them, doing the calculation on his phone. Takis not only didn’t have a cent, he had no intention of paying even if he did. This, admittedly, also forced him to go without a bank account, but that’s another matter. Takis was into the barter economy and “off the grid living,” as he called it. He watched videos of guys in America who had withdrawn to the middle of nowhere, to some forests in Canada, built a cabin powered by solar energy, drew water from wells, serious stuff, and still had satellite Internet. Takis, of course, had done none of this. He just lived in the house their mother had left him, had stopped paying water bills, and stole electricity from the neighbor. The Greek edition of “off the grid.”

In the fourth year of Geology and the eighteenth of Mathematics, Chrysoula got a job as a secretarial assistant at a company that imported clothing from China into Thessaloniki. “Thessalochina” was the shop’s name, run by a Chinese man with the sense of humor of Seferlis, only Chinese. The company was doing fairly well but had one main problem: the sizes, whatever you call them. Let me explain. For an average Thessalonian to find trousers in his size at “Thessalochina,” he had to start at 3XL and go up. With a rough model Chrysoula put together (not a geological one, a mathematical one), the sizes should have started at XL and gone up to 21XL for the business to work in this city. Chrysoula showed them the statistics, but they weren’t much persuaded, the owner must have been the only Chinese person who knew no Mathematics at all. So she decided on something more decisive. She convinced the customers who came in and didn’t buy, because there were no sizes for them, to go to the Chinese man, whose office was on the mezzanine, and tell him they would buy if he had something in their size. One, two, three, thirty of them, and the Chinese owner, statistically unpersuadable as Plato might have put it, finally gave in. Now they had sizes even for the largest Thessalonian, whether along the x axis or the y axis.

The idea was a huge success and, as a reward, the Chinese owners made her manager of the Thessaloniki store. “Manager, ‘Thessalochina’ Clothing Store,” read her business card. As a sign of gratitude, the director, the Chinese Seferlis Zhao Hui/Vangelis (he had two names, one for each country), even let her buy two percent of the business dirt cheap, in tribute to the idea that was making them richer by the day. With this poor but honest two percent, a decent salary and a lot of thrift, Chrysoula managed within five years to pay off her share of the debt and also lent her brother fifteen thousand euros. In reality she didn’t lend it to him, since the hopeless case had no account, she simply paid fifteen thousand euros out of his share, and he promised her he would find the rest to pay. Bullshit, obviously. Where would he find it? Eighteen months went by, and the bank had received no payment. Chrysoula worried both about her brother and about their family house, which they owned jointly in equal shares and of which her brother’s part had been mortgaged for the settlement, in case at least fifty percent of the amount wasn’t paid within two years of reaching the settlement. Chrysoula believed (naively, obviously) that seven and a half thousand euros in two years comes to two hundred and fifty euros a month, my brother will manage somehow, she thought, even though she knew his income had stayed at zero for the last ten years. Within two years the bank had authorized its good friends, the collection agencies, to threaten foreclosure. Chrysoula’s brother told them every time, eleven in all: “Fuck your Virgin Mary!” and hung up on them. “I’m not paying to listen to their bullshit,” he then told Mrs. Soula, who ran the café that had a phone (like the whole village, except the off-grid guy).

But one fine day the bank decided to get a prosecutor’s order and send the same order to the collection agency, whose employee had taken every blasphemy against the Virgin Mary of his life and so had a sort of personal beef, because religious “beliefs” are personal and when someone insults them you’re personally insulted too (okay, you have to be a bit of a jerk for that to happen). As a result, the agency took four burly thugs, a prosecutor’s order, and a pretentious dandy of an employee who had taken about fifty-four percent of the Virgin Marys and sent them to seize the house. Chrysoula’s brother was shouting something unintelligible like “come and take them” and pointing at the biceps he didn’t have, the neighbors, men and women, were trying to talk the thugs and the fop round, generally, a difficult scene. One of the neighbor women, tremendously enlightened compared to Chrysoula’s brother (she had gone through twelve and a half Enlightenments more than him), decided Chrysoula had to be told. “Chrysoula is sensible, she’s no blockhead like this one here.”

“Chrysoula, it’s Vasilo. Some gallows birds have come with a dandy and they want to get into the house, they say they’re going to seize it.”

“Vasilo dear, try to stop them. Talk to them reasonably. Put me on to them if you have to. If they get in, I’ll be co-owner of the property with the bastards.”

“I’ll do something, girl, don’t you worry.”

“I’ll be there in a few hours.”

Near Malgara, Chrysoula got a call from the dandy.

“I’m the sister of the gentleman who owes you. Wait two hours, I’m coming to settle it. Be patient. Have a little coffee next door, at Vasilo’s café, and I’ll be there.”

“Who’s paying for the coffees?” asked the dandy.

“My treat.”

The dandy thought it a good deal. He’d have coffee, eat a cheese pie or two, wait a while, and maybe even pick up a few bucks. Fine by him. Vasilo watched him with contempt. Look at the kind of people who run our lives! she thought. The dandy sipped his double Greek coffee at the pace of cod liver oil. By the time Chrysoula arrived, he hadn’t even finished half of it, would you believe.

“Chrysoula Gouda, sister of Ioannis Gouda, we spoke on the phone.”

“Right, go ahead.”

“So, I have a proposal for you: I pay you half of the total amount now, and we call it quits. The other option is for you to take on my half-crazy brother and, if you manage, to co-own a house in the condition you see.”

“That can’t be done, ma’am. The bank demands the full amount.”

“Take them, and tell me. Either you leave with half or you get, let’s not say what. I’m not prepared to give you a euro more. And it’s plenty for you as it is, you bastards. Shh, don’t react, I’m saying it right.”

“Easy, what would I react to, because you call us bastards, we hear it every day. Though if I could, I’d tear your hair out, you hag.”

“Your mother, you clown.”

“Yours.”

“She’s dead, you buffoon, that’s why the house is ours, what don’t you understand? Ten years now. And six for my dad.”

“I see. Have you dug her up, to take the bones to the ossuary? I did it a few days ago for my mom.”

“Forgive me. I didn’t know. I’m sorry. About your mom, I mean.”

“It’s all right. Go see what’s happening, if they haven’t notified you, they will soon. Mine hadn’t decomposed, hadn’t rotted, the lady from the funeral parlor said. Never mind, it’s a drama.”

“I’m sorry again. I’ll take care of it.”

“So, seven and a half thousand. The bank accepts. I’m sending you a link with the agreement.”

Chrysoula looks at Vasilo and waves a soothing hand, as if to say “we’ll sort it out.” Her brother asks what happened, and Chrysoula tells him that his “Molon Labe” stance scared them and they’re going to write off the debts.

“If they wanted, they could have done it differently. Sis, will you buy me a tsipouro?”

“I’m buying, leave your sister alone, she’s tired,” says Vasilo.

He sits down. Chrysoula sits next to him. She opens her tablet. Looks at the agreement. Reads. Finds everything just as it had been agreed. Signs. Sends it to the dandy.

“Received,” says the dandy.

He signs. Done. Nobody owes anything to anybody. Except the soil. That may still owe something to Chrysoula’s mom.

The next day, almost at dawn, she called Koula, the head of the area’s funeral parlor, who in turn took the two burly guys she kept for burying and digging up people, and they set off for the cemetery.

“There’s no way your mom has decomposed,” said Koula. “The ground is inhospitable, disdainful.”

Chrysoula said nothing. The hulks grumbled and dug. They hit wood. They opened it. Her mom looked them straight in the eyes. It was as if she’d been buried a few hours ago. She looked happy to see them. Then again, that’s how she had looked ten years earlier, when they were burying her. Chrysoula began to tremble. Koula took her by the arm and sat her down on the bench. She explained again that this is how it is. The ground is like that, cursed, inhospitable. She cried for a bit but pulled herself together quickly.

“So what do we do now? Mom has to be exhumed, doesn’t she?”

“Don’t worry, they’ll give us a few days’ grace.”

“What will happen in a few days? A person doesn’t decompose that fast.”

“I don’t know what you mean, but if you mean she dissolves, you’re wrong, it can be done. Six kilos of bleach. Two days. Twelve kilos, one day at most!”

“Holy Mother, help me! What bleach? Pour bleach on my mom?”

“Not on your mom, for heaven’s sake! On the soil. We’re not barbarians. If you have another solution, I’m listening.”

She thought about it for a while. Shouldn’t she ask her brother too? On the other hand, what advice could she get from that man? She might as well ask her dead mother herself rather than him. She asked her:

“Mom, I’m sorry, tell me: what should I do?”

Her mom smiled at her. Chrysoula took heart.

“Let’s do it. But I’ll take part too.”

“Fine. A hundred euros including the bleach. Payment up front.”

They didn’t have to go down to buy any. Koula had a stock of bleach in the hearse. She brought a dozen. She started pouring it onto the soil where the mom was. Chrysoula watched for a moment the path the bleach followed before the ground absorbed it. She wasn’t satisfied with the topology and the efficiency of the undertaking.

An important contribution of Algebraic Topology is the optimization of transport systems, thought Chrysoula.

She took the bleach out of Koula’s hands.

“Leave it, I’ll do it.”

She began pouring the bleach in a pattern reminiscent of topological diagram representations. The soil had ceased to be a homogeneous volume. It had been transformed into a space with a topological structure of its own, a network of paths, nodes and the connections between them.

“Do you see these small hollows, Mrs. Koula? Like branching points in a graph. If we pour the bleach here and here, we’ll optimize the flow through the network…” She paused briefly, took a breath and continued: “… Basically, it’s the traveling salesman problem in reverse, upside down. Instead of looking for the shortest route that passes through all the points, you look for a way to cover the entire space with the minimum possible quantity of liquid. A simple graph covering problem, my dear Mrs. Koula, I can’t believe you hadn’t seen it all this time!”

Koula, and the burly guys with her, looked at her with bewilderment and perhaps a touch of terror. To them all of this sounded like Aramaic, at best. Chrysoula went on methodically, almost ritually, pouring the bleach. Every so often she stopped and observed how it was absorbed by the soil, adjusting the model in real time.

“Forgive me, Mom!” she whispered. “Then again, you always said we should do our jobs properly and finish whatever we start. That’s what I’m doing too.”

Koula took a cigarette from one of the guys. She lit it and watched the process with genuine curiosity.

“First time I’ve seen a method like this. Usually we pour the bleach any which way, wherever it lands.”

“Nothing is any which way, wherever it lands. Not the soil and not death.”
